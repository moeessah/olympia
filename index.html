The idea: a conversation with Olympia can lead to meeting someone new. When something a person says points to someone they’d enjoy talking to, Olympia suggests one person, asks both, makes the hello, then steps away.

Working assumption: Introductions comes after Olympia is in more homes. A supervised pilot with 100–500 people first, then a wider pilot of 50k–100k users once it proves itself.

01 · THE PROBLEM

(The assumption I would test first: a daily chat with Olympia and a hello from a person each give something different.)

Someone can talk to Olympia every day and still have almost no human contact.

Family may live far away. Friends have moved, become less mobile, or died. Retirement and bereavement quietly shrink the number of reasons to leave the house or pick up the phone.

Loneliness is personal, so Olympia listens and leaves the diagnosing to others. When something a person says points to a chance to reach out, she offers it once, gently.

How I decided: notes on the family app

The promise

Olympia is a voice-first companion in an older adult's home. This app is the family's screen. It tells them when something needs attention and leaves the day's conversations with Margaret. Every screen carries one promise: peace of mind that keeps her privacy whole.

Three decisions that shaped it

  • Memory Book belongs to Margaret. The stories are in her own words and she decides what is kept, by voice. Family reads and listens, and she remains the only author.
  • Alerts record who reached out. Each card says who acted and how, with their initial. Read receipts would turn a calm app into a scoreboard between siblings, so the record lists who reached out. Who looked stays private. It is permanent because three people share it, and anything older than a week settles into History.
  • Olympia always says what she is doing. A wellness check moves from "Olympia is checking in" to either "Margaret is fine" or "Couldn't reach her", and a failure always offers Try again and Call Margaret, so the family has a next step in every state.
More decisions
  • Recording is off by default. If someone separately agrees to a recording, it stays off this screen too. Today is a signal, and Margaret's words stay between her and the people she talks to.
  • Alerts keeps its plain name. Alerts is the one tab someone must find fast in an emergency, so speed beats a calmer name like "Updates."
  • The badge lights for urgent alone. A badge promises that something deserves an interruption. Reserving it for urgent keeps that promise believable, and amber items wait inside the tab.
  • Call Margaret lives on Today. Inside an alert it would suggest that calling is for emergencies. On Today it says a check-in is something families do on any day.
  • Introductions lives inside Today. A tab would frame it as a signal that rises and falls. Inside Today it informs, and the wording says what happened and stops there.
  • Family splits into two decisions. My notifications affect only me. Who has access affects Margaret and the whole family, so adding someone routes through her approval.
  • One trust explainer, in Settings. "Urgent always reaches family, and Margaret is told at the same moment" should be findable in one place.
  • Worth a look items open a note. Wellness check and mark as handled are reserved for urgent alerts on purpose.
  • Wellness check is per person; mark as handled is per alert. Two urgent incidents are two things to acknowledge, but only one check-in with Margaret should be in flight. Requests need a confirm step, and a cooldown after each result.
  • Handled asks how, in one tap. Marking handled opens a short sheet: a quick pick, and an optional note. The card then reads "David called her", so the next sibling knows whether to act. Notes are for family only for now.
  • Escalation is visible step by step. If a care contact is set and Margaret doesn't answer, Olympia calls them and the family sees calling, answered or didn't answer, then done. If they don't answer either, the next move is the family's: call them, call Margaret, or try again.
  • A push opens the alert it is about. Tapping an urgent push opens that alert, and a wellness result opens the alert that asked. Neither drops you on the list.
  • The care contact is set by the Organizer, seen by everyone. It puts a third party in the loop about Margaret, so like adding a person, it is one decision for the whole family and Margaret is told who it is. Members can see who Olympia will try next, so nobody is surprised by the call. They cannot change it.
  • Anyone with access can mark an alert handled. No approval step in an emergency. Only the Organizer can add or remove people, and Margaret can remove anyone by telling Olympia. Two roles only, Organizer and Member.
  • Settings is one gear. The Family tab holds the circle (people, roles, care contact). The gear opens My settings, which are personal: alert cadence, account, help.
  • Today shows how she is doing. Last spoke with Olympia, a good time to call, something to ask about from her own Memory Book, and who last called. Each one helps family connect. None of them says what she said.
  • The intro says the promise before anything else. Three screens: know she is okay, hear if something is wrong, and she stays in charge.
  • Tab says Memories, header says Memory Book. Four tabs leave no room for the full name. A guess to test at real sizes with real users.
  • Severity is a fine outline and a tinted chip. Red marks urgent and amber marks worth a look, on plain cards like everything else. Handled turns green and quiet.
  • Time zones travel with the family. When the viewer is abroad, Today adds a line such as "6:40pm where Margaret is".

What the research says

  • Most caregiver apps are care hubs: task lists, vitals, shared logs. Reviews frame the real problem as handoffs, where information gets lost between people. Olympia Family stays small and answers one question well.
  • Older adults and their adult children describe privacy in similar terms and weigh monitoring differently. Older adults will trade some privacy for independence when they help decide (Gerontologist, 2020).
  • One fall-detection pilot ended when the woman it protected switched it off after false alarms brought unannounced visits. Alerts that cry wolf lose the home, so severity stays rare and trustworthy.
  • In week one I would sit with an older adult at home, watch how she responds when Olympia mentions a family check-in, and rework this section around what I see.

Visual system

402 x 874 frame, SF Pro, 4 pt spacing, 44 pt tap targets. Green marks anything tappable. Red and amber are reserved for alert severity, and handled states always pair colour with a tick or a word. A studio owns your visual identity, so this stays to what the prototype needed.

Still open

  • The 9 second cooldown and the confirm step are my best guesses, and real users will show where they belong.
  • Push suppression checks the open alert screen only. A backgrounded app or a second device would need its own signal.
  • Every resolution in the prototype (Margaret, the care contact) is a timer standing in for a real conversation or call, driven by the demo controls.
  • Access shows who can see Margaret's world and treats what each person is told as one setting. A son who wants falls without mood dips has no way to say so yet, and I would hold that until families show which categories they want to split.
  • Handled notes are family-only, with four quick picks (Called her, Spoke to a neighbour, Visited, No action needed) and an optional one-line note. Open question: should Margaret see them?
  • Should family ever suggest a Memory Book topic? It could work like the wellness check, with Olympia raising it and Margaret unaware of who asked. The risk is added pressure on a book that is meant to be hers.

05 · PILOT: LEARN BEFORE YOU SCALE

100–500

people in a curated early cohort

The first group only needs to be large enough to get a handful of matches right.

The first test is a service test. Before anyone is matched, older adults help shape the language, how permission gets asked for, matching rules and failure cases. Then a small supervised cohort tries the whole thing, start to finish. Calls are recorded only if someone separately agrees.

Co-design before build

Create a small lived-experience council of around 8–12 older adults before the service is built. Arlene Astell, one of your advisors, is a leading expert in co-creating ageing technology with older people. This council borrows the same idea. Work through short interviews, role-play, prototype run-throughs and failure cases. Give participants prototypes, scripts and failure cases early enough that their comments can change the requirements.

Bring together people with different ages, living situations, digital confidence, communication needs, cultural backgrounds and experiences of social connection. Include people who already have rich social lives alongside people who sometimes feel isolated.

THE USEFUL TEST

A prototype passes this test when comments like these change something:

OVERSHARING“Why does Olympia need to know that?”Take that information out.
AWKWARD ASK“I would find that question awkward.”Rewrite the question.
WANTS OUT“I would want an easier way to stop this.”Strengthen the stop rule.
Evidence

After the first call, did both people independently want another one?

It only counts when both people say, privately, that they’d want another one. A starting target to agree with the council: at least 7 in 10 pairs say yes to a second call.

THE OVERRIDE
STOP THE MATCH
A safety, identity, consent or privacy concern sends it back to review. The other person hears that it didn’t go ahead.

The reviewer can stop a match on safety grounds. Beyond that, the choice to meet belongs to the person.

09 · BEFORE YOU GO

A note for you

I keep coming back to one question: what could Olympia do with the relationship she already has? She could put two people in touch, through a voice they already trust, and give each of them someone they might enjoy talking with.

I keep thinking about my grandmother on the phone with her sister. As they got older and moving around got harder, they travelled less and phoned each other more. They still had so much to gossip about. Nobody stops wanting to be heard. Introductions is a phone people already love, ringing.

I put this together for Harmonica. I'd like to talk it through, and spend my first week sitting with your users.
