Designing features
for people
who would rather just talk

Two prototypes, about ten minutes. Olympia Family is a companion app for relatives. Introductions is the feature I would build next: Olympia suggests one other Olympia user, asks each person separately, makes the hello, and steps away. No profile to fill in, nobody to browse. It needs enough users to work, so it comes second.

01 · THE PROBLEM

(Same loneliness, two views. This is the family’s. The first number I’d ask you for: of people who talk to Olympia daily, how many spoke to another person that day?)

Someone can talk to Olympia every day and still have almost no human contact.

Family may live far away. Friends move, slow down, or die. Retirement and bereavement take away the reasons to leave the house or pick up the phone.

Loneliness is personal, so Olympia does not diagnose it. When something a person says points to someone they could reach, she offers once and lets it go.

The family app is also where the first step of Introductions lives: “Would you like me to call your daughter?”

Build the first real hello.

Social platforms make the person do the work: open an app, write a profile, browse strangers, message, coordinate. Olympia already has their trust and their voice, so the hello comes through her.

PRECEDENT
Age UK, Call in Time

Free weekly half-hour calls from a volunteer for people over 60, matched on shared interests through a sign-up profile.

What is different here

Call in Time pairs a volunteer with an older person on a fixed schedule. Introductions pairs two Olympia users, using what Olympia already knows and what each has confirmed. No profile to fill in.

How I decided: notes on the family app

The promise

Olympia is a voice-first companion in an older adult's home. This app is the family's screen: it tells them when something needs attention and leaves the day's conversations with Margaret. Every screen carries one promise, peace of mind with her privacy intact.

Three decisions that shaped it

  • Memory Book belongs to Margaret. The stories are in her own words and she decides what is kept, by voice. Family reads and listens. She stays the only author.
  • Alerts record who reached out. Each card says who acted and how. Read receipts would turn a calm app into a scoreboard between siblings, so the app records who reached out and keeps who looked private. Anything older than a week settles into History.
  • Olympia always says what she is doing. A wellness check moves from "Olympia is checking in" to "Margaret is fine" or "Couldn't reach her", and a failure always offers Try again and Call Margaret. Every state has a next step.
More decisions
  • The badge lights for urgent alone. A badge promises something deserves an interruption. Reserving it for urgent keeps that promise believable. Amber items wait inside the tab.
  • Call Margaret lives on Today. Inside an alert it would suggest calling is for emergencies. On Today it says a check-in is something families do on any day.
  • Introductions lives inside Today. A tab would frame it as a signal that rises and falls. Inside Today it just informs: it says what happened and stops.
  • Family splits into two decisions. My notifications affect only me. Who has access affects Margaret and the whole family, so adding someone needs her approval.
  • Escalation is visible step by step. If Margaret doesn't answer and a care contact is set, Olympia calls them and the family sees each step: calling, answered or not, done. If they don't answer either, the next move is the family's: call them, call Margaret, or try again.
  • Today shows how she is doing. Last spoke with Olympia, a good time to call, something to ask about from her own Memory Book, and who last called. Each helps family connect. None says what she said.

What the research says

  • Most caregiver apps are care hubs: task lists, vitals, shared logs. In the caregiver-app reviews I read, the real problem is handoffs, where information gets lost between people. Olympia Family is small on purpose and answers one question well.
  • Older adults and their adult children describe privacy in similar terms but weigh monitoring differently. In a small US study of 18 mothers and 10 of their adult children, the children liked each monitoring technology more than their mothers did and underestimated how much say the mothers should have (Berridge and Wetle, The Gerontologist, 2020). That is why Margaret approves who has access.
  • Alerts that cry wolf lose the home, so severity has to be rare to be believed.
  • In week one I would sit with an older adult at home, watch how she responds when Olympia mentions a family check-in, and rework this section around what I see.

Visual system

Green marks anything tappable. Red and amber are reserved for alert severity, and handled states pair colour with a tick or a word. A studio owns your visual identity, so I kept the system to what the prototype needed.

Still open

  • The 9 second cooldown and the confirm step are my best guesses, and real users will show where they belong.
  • Every resolution in the prototype (Margaret, the care contact) is a timer standing in for a real conversation or call.
  • Handled notes are family-only. Open question: should Margaret see them?
  • Should family ever suggest a Memory Book topic? Olympia would tell Margaret who asked and she could say no, but it still adds pressure to a book meant to be hers. I would leave it out until families ask.

04 · PILOT: LEARN BEFORE YOU SCALE

30

pairs, about 60 people, in one language and time zone

The first test is a service test. Before anyone is matched, older adults help shape the wording, how permission is asked, the matching rules and the failure cases. Then a small supervised cohort runs the whole thing start to finish. Calls are recorded only if someone separately agrees.

  1. Council8–12 people
  2. Gate300–400 users
  3. Pilot30 pairs
  4. Evidence7 in 10 want another call

Matching does not start before the gate, whatever the calendar says. The square marks that: a threshold, not a step.

Co-design before build

Form a council of 8 to 12 older adults before the service is built. Arlene Astell, one of your scientific advisors, co-creates ageing and dementia technology with older people, and this borrows her approach. Use short interviews, role-play and prototype run-throughs, and show people the scripts and failure cases early enough that their comments can change the requirements.

Recruit for range: different living situations, digital confidence and hearing, and some people who are not lonely at all.

THE USEFUL TEST

A prototype passes this test when comments like these change something:

OVERSHARING“Why does Olympia need to know that?”Take that information out.
AWKWARD ASK“I would find that question awkward.”Rewrite the question.
WANTS OUT“I would want an easier way to stop this.”Strengthen the stop rule.
Evidence

After the first call, did both people independently want another one?

Read 7 in 10 as a threshold to agree with the council. Below about 30 pairs it starts a conversation and proves nothing.

08 · BEFORE YOU GO

A note for you

Paolo, your post says you are building an interface that learns the person. What Olympia learns first is what someone loves talking about, and the best person to share that with is often another Olympia user. Introductions is that step: one suggestion, both people asked separately, a hello, and then she leaves.

The family app is the part I would ship first, and families are a big part of how Olympia grows. Emma can check on her mum without being invasive. The care of an older adult is shared between relatives instead of falling to the favourite child. And they can see their mother is being social beyond the family, without hearing what she said.

I keep thinking about my grandmother on the phone with her sister. As moving around got harder they travelled less and phoned more, and still had so much to gossip about. Introductions is a phone people already love, ringing.

I built the family app to show Introductions in context, and as the other arm of what technology can do: keep someone company, and help them find company.
